Lhasa

The Roof of the World — Where Faith Meets the Sky

Core Scenic Spots

Potala Palace (Budala Gong)

Location: Red Hill (Marpo Ri), central Lhasa, rising 3,700 meters above sea level

Historical Background: The Potala Palace is the most recognizable building in Tibet and the spiritual landmark of Lhasa — a monumental white-and-maroon fortress-palace that climbs the slopes of Red Hill in cascading tiers of wall, ramp, and golden roof. Its story begins in the 7th century, when King Songtsen Gampo built a first palace here for his bride, the Tang Princess Wencheng. That early structure was largely lost to fire and warfare, and the palace we see today was rebuilt from 1645 onward under the Fifth Dalai Lama, who made it the winter seat of Tibet's government and the residence of successive Dalai Lamas. Standing 13 stories and 117 meters tall, with more than 1,000 rooms, tens of thousands of statues, and walls painted with some 10,000 square meters of murals, it is the world's highest and largest ancient palace-fortress. It was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994, together with the Jokhang Temple and Norbulingka.

Highlights:

  • The White Palace (Kharpo Podrang) — the administrative and residential wing, with grand audience halls and the living quarters of the Dalai Lamas
  • The Red Palace (Marpo Podrang) — the religious heart, housing the gilded burial stupas of eight Dalai Lamas, including the Fifth Dalai Lama's magnificent 14.85-meter gold stupa
  • The Golden Roofs — a cluster of gleaming gilded roofs crowning the Red Palace, visible across the city
  • The Great Murals — corridors and halls covered in centuries-old frescoes depicting Tibetan history, Buddhist cosmology, and the lives of the saints
  • The West Great Hall and Chapel of the Dalai Lamas' tombs — the most sacred chambers, where pilgrims prostrate and add yak butter to the eternal lamps

Opening Hours: 9:00 AM – 4:00 PM (last entry ~3:00 PM); closed to interior visits on some maintenance days — check in advance

Tickets: ¥200 (peak season, May 1 – October 31); ¥100 (off season). Daily visitor numbers are strictly capped (about 2,300), and tickets must be reserved in advance — typically 1–7 days ahead, via the official platform or a licensed agency.

Transportation: A 15–20 minute walk from Barkhor Square; bus routes 2, 6, 13, 17, 24 to the Potala Palace stop; taxi from anywhere in the old city about ¥10–15

Best Duration: 2–3 hours (the climb involves many steep stairs at high altitude — go slowly)

Best Season: May–October, when the skies are clearest and the altitude is most manageable; the palace is stunning at dawn and at dusk when the facade is floodlit

Tips: Reserve your ticket as early as possible — same-day entry is rare. Climb very slowly and drink water, as the stairs at 3,700 meters leave even fit visitors breathless. Photography is forbidden inside the palace, so keep your camera packed once past the gates. Dress respectfully and remove your hat when entering the chapels. The classic panorama photo is taken from the Chagpori viewing platform across the square.

Jokhang Temple (Da Zhao Si)

Location: Barkhor Square, the very center of old Lhasa

Historical Background: The Jokhang Temple is the holiest site in Tibetan Buddhism — the spiritual heart to which every pilgrim in Tibet hopes to travel at least once in a lifetime. Built in the 7th century by King Songtsen Gampo, it was raised to house two sacred statues brought as dowries by his two wives: the Jowo Mikyo Dorje from his Nepali bride Princess Bhrikuti, and, most famously, the Jowo Shakyamuni — a gilded image of the Buddha at age twelve — brought by the Tang Princess Wencheng from Chang'an (today's Xi'an). Over the centuries the temple grew from a small shrine into a sprawling complex of chapels, courtyards, and golden roofs, blending Tibetan, Nepali, Indian, and Tang Chinese architectural styles. It was listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1994. For Tibetans, the Jokhang is not a museum but a living sanctuary — pilgrims prostrate outside its gates from dawn to dusk, and the air is thick with juniper incense, yak-butter lamps, and murmured mantras.

Highlights:

  • The Jowo Shakyamuni statue — Tibet's most venerated image of the Buddha, covered in gold and jewels and tended with offerings by monks
  • The Golden Roof — a magnificent gilded roof rising above the central hall, best admired from the rooftop terrace
  • The Rooftop Terrace — climb up for sweeping views over Barkhor Square and, in the distance, the Potala Palace
  • The Circumambulation Corridor (Nangkhor) — an inner prayer circuit lined with hundreds of spinning brass prayer wheels
  • The Pilgrims' Courtyard — where the faithful perform full-body prostrations, a deeply moving sight of devotion

Opening Hours: 9:00 AM – 6:00 PM daily; mornings are the most atmospheric, when pilgrims arrive to offer prayers and butter

Tickets: ¥85 (combined entrance); the Barkhor circuit around it is free

Transportation: In the heart of the old city, a short walk from most central hotels; bus routes 2, 5, 8, 12 to Barkhor; taxi from anywhere central under ¥15

Best Duration: 1.5–2 hours (interior) plus time to linger on the rooftop and observe the pilgrims

Best Season: Year-round; early mornings are the most serene and spiritual, before tour groups arrive

Tips: Always walk clockwise around the temple and its corridors, as pilgrims do. Do not photograph worshippers at close range without permission, and never photograph the Jowo statue or inside the chapels. Combine with Barkhor Street — the two together form the soul of old Lhasa. A visit at first light, amid the juniper smoke and prostration boards, is one of the most moving experiences in Asia.

Barkhor Street (Ba Kuo Jie)

Location: A circular street ringing the Jokhang Temple, the historic center of Lhasa

Historical Background: Barkhor Street is the oldest street in Lhasa and the beating heart of the old city — a ring-shaped circuit that has served for over 1,300 years as both a marketplace and a sacred pilgrimage path. The name means "middle circumambulation," for the street is the kora, or devotional walking circuit, around the Jokhang Temple, trodden clockwise by a ceaseless stream of pilgrims who spin brass prayer wheels and murmur mantras as they go. Around this living mandala grew the bazaar that supplied the city: stalls and shops selling thangka paintings, turquoise and coral jewelry, yak-butter lamps, prayer flags, incense, carpets, and the bright cotton aprons worn by Tibetan women. The surrounding lanes preserve traditional whitewashed Tibetan houses with black-framed windows and rooftop prayer flags. Walking Barkhor Street is to join a river of faith and commerce that has flowed, essentially unchanged, since the time of Songtsen Gampo.

Highlights:

  • The Pilgrims' Kora — join the clockwise flow of worshippers, prostrators, and elderly Tibetans turning their prayer wheels
  • Traditional Shops — browse thangkas, turquoise, yak-bone carvings, prayer flags, and handwoven textiles
  • Sweet Tea Houses — slip into a low-ceilinged tea house (like the beloved Guangming Tea House) for sweet milk tea and people-watching
  • Old Tibetan Architecture — the surrounding lanes of whitewashed, black-windowed houses are some of Lhasa's best-preserved heritage
  • The Rooftop Cafés — many teahouses and cafés offer terraces with golden views of the Jokhang's roof and the Potala beyond

Opening Hours: Open all day; shops generally 9:00 AM – 9:00 PM; the pilgrims' circuit is liveliest at dawn and dusk

Tickets: Free (a public street); the Jokhang Temple interior requires a ticket

Transportation: In the very center of Lhasa; most central hotels are within walking distance; bus routes 2, 5, 8, 12; taxi from anywhere central under ¥15

Best Duration: 2–3 hours for a leisurely circuit, shopping, and a tea-house stop

Best Season: Year-round; early morning and evening offer the most atmospheric light and the fullest flow of pilgrims

Tips: Always walk clockwise with the pilgrims — walking against the flow is considered disrespectful. Ask before photographing people, especially those prostrating. Bargain gently in the shops, and be aware that many "antiques" are modern reproductions. Duck into the side alleys for the most authentic tea houses and workshops, away from the souvenir crowds.

Sera Monastery (Se La Si)

Location: At the foot of Sera Uste Hill, about 5 km north of central Lhasa

Historical Background: Sera Monastery is one of the "Three Great" Gelugpa monasteries of Lhasa — alongside Ganden and Drepung — and one of Tibet's greatest centers of Buddhist learning. It was founded in 1419 by Jamchen Chöje, a disciple of the great reformer Tsongkhapa, and at its height housed more than 5,000 monks across its three residential colleges and numerous chapels. The sprawling white-and-ochre complex clings to the hillside, its halls crowded with centuries of murals, gilded statues, and a famous collection of thangkas and scriptures. But Sera is celebrated above all for its tradition of monastic debate — the daily sessions in which young monks, in pairs, deliver explosive claps and emphatic lunges as they challenge one another on Buddhist philosophy. This centuries-old method of rigorous, physical argument is the monastery's living heart and one of the most extraordinary spectacles in Tibet.

Highlights:

  • The Debating Courtyard — the famous afternoon debates (weekdays), where robed monks clap, stamp, and lunge in animated philosophical argument
  • The Great Assembly Hall — the monastery's main hall, filled with murals, thangkas, and a colossal gilded Maitreya (future Buddha) statue
  • The Three Colleges — Sera Me, Sera Je, and Ngakpa, each with its own chapels and traditions of scholarship
  • The Maitreya Chapel — housing one of Lhasa's most venerated images of the future Buddha
  • The Kora Path — a peaceful circuit around the monastery walls, offering hillside views and quieter moments of contemplation

Opening Hours: 9:00 AM – 4:00 PM daily; the famous debates are held roughly 3:00–5:00 PM on weekdays (Monday to Friday)

Tickets: ¥50

Transportation: Bus routes 16, 24, 25 from the city center; taxi about ¥20–25 (15 minutes) from Barkhor

Best Duration: 2–3 hours (arrive around 2:30 PM to see the debates, then explore the halls)

Best Season: Year-round; the debates run on weekdays throughout the year, and the hillside setting is especially beautiful in summer

Tips: Time your visit for mid-afternoon on a weekday to catch the debates — they are the monastery's must-see. Photography of the debates is generally permitted from the edges of the courtyard, but avoid flash and be unobtrusive. Walk clockwise through the halls and kora. Allow extra time in traffic, as the road north of the city can be slow.

Featured Cuisine

Tsampa (糌粑 / Roasted Barley Flour)

Flavor Profile: The foundation of Tibetan cuisine — a nutty, faintly sweet flour made from highland barley (qingke) that is roasted, then ground into a fine powder. At the table, a handful of tsampa is kneaded in a wooden bowl with yak butter tea or plain tea, sometimes sweetened with sugar or enriched with crumbled dried cheese (chura), and worked with the fingers into a dense, pliable dough that is rolled into small balls. The result is earthy, satisfying, and deeply sustaining — a mouthful that tastes of the barley fields and high, clean air of the plateau. It needs no cooking and no fire, which is precisely the point: tsampa is food you can carry anywhere, and its warm, toasty flavor is the taste of Tibet itself.

Historical & Cultural Story: For centuries tsampa has been the staple that fueled Tibetan nomads, monks, and caravan travelers across the roof of the world. Because it keeps for months and needs only a splash of tea to prepare, it was the perfect ration for herders following yak across the grasslands and for pilgrims walking to distant monasteries. Its centrality is such that "eating tsampa" is nearly synonymous with being Tibetan. It is the first food offered to a guest, the base of every meal, and a symbol of hospitality and identity. In monasteries, monks begin the day with a bowl of tsampa and butter tea before prayers, and nomads still carry it in a leather pouch tied to the saddle.

How to Eat:

  1. Knead a handful with yak butter tea in a wooden bowl until it forms a dough
  2. Roll it into small balls with your fingers and eat from the hand
  3. Add sugar or dried cheese (chura) for a richer, sweeter version
  4. Take it slowly — it is dense and filling, and meant to be savored with tea

Price Range: ¥5–15 for a bowl at a tea house; a bag to take home costs ¥20–50

Where to Try:

  • Traditional Tibetan restaurants in the Barkhor area — order tsampa alongside yak butter tea
  • Sweet tea houses (甜茶馆) — many serve tsampa as a staple snack
  • Family-run guesthouses — the most authentic way, kneaded fresh by the hosts

Momo (藏式馍馍 / Tibetan Dumplings)

Flavor Profile: Tibet's beloved steamed dumplings — plump, juicy parcels of minced yak meat wrapped in a delicate, thin skin and steamed until tender. The filling is seasoned with onion, garlic, ginger, cilantro, and a whisper of Sichuan pepper for warmth, giving each bite a fragrant, savory depth. Vegetarian versions are equally common, filled with potato, soft cheese (churu), or cabbage. Momo are served by the steaming basketful, always accompanied by a fiery tomato-chili chutney (sepen) for dipping, which cuts through the richness with a bright, hot kick. The contrast of the chewy skin, the juicy filling, and the scorching chutney is the dish's simple, irresistible genius.

Historical & Cultural Story: Momo arrived in Tibet along the ancient caravan routes that linked Lhasa with Kathmandu, a delicious gift of the region's long Himalayan trade with Nepal. Over the centuries, the Nepali original adapted to the plateau, swapping in yak meat and taking on the warming spices that suit the cold. Today momo are a fixture of Tibetan life — the comfort food of the plateau, shared communally at family tables, sold from street stalls, and prepared in vast quantities for Losar (Tibetan New Year) and other festivals. They are the dish that unites monks, nomads, and city-dwellers alike: humble, portable, and endlessly reassuring.

How to Eat:

  1. Lift a momo from the steamer with your chopsticks and dip it generously into the sepen chutney
  2. Eat it in one or two bites to catch the juices while hot
  3. Try both yak-meat and vegetarian fillings to appreciate the range
  4. Order a basket to share — momo are meant to be communal

Price Range: ¥15–30 for a basket of 8–10; street-stall portions are cheaper

Where to Try:

  • Tibetan restaurants around Barkhor Street — the most reliable, time-tested versions
  • Street stalls and small eateries near the Jokhang — for a quick, cheap, authentic bite
  • Family guesthouses — many hosts steam momo fresh for breakfast or dinner

Yak Butter Tea (酥油茶 / Po Cha)

Flavor Profile: The defining drink of Tibet — a warm, savory, slightly salty brew of black tea, yak butter, and salt, churned together in a tall wooden cylinder until emulsified into a rich, pale, almost soupy tea. The flavor is unlike anything else: the earthy, tannic bite of strong tea is rounded and mellowed by the creamy, faintly smoky yak butter, with a gentle saltiness that is strangely comforting at altitude. It is an acquired taste for many visitors, but on a cold plateau morning, nothing warms the body or steadies the spirit quite like it. Butter tea is not a refreshment — it is fuel, and Tibetans drink it constantly, cup after refilled cup, all day long.

Historical & Cultural Story: Butter tea is inseparable from Tibetan life. On a plateau where the cold saps energy and the body burns calories just to stay warm, the blend of tea's caffeine, butter's fat, and salt's electrolytes became the perfect high-altitude sustenance. It is brewed from compressed black-tea bricks traded along the ancient Tea Horse Road from Yunnan and Sichuan, then churned with yak butter and salt in a tradition that has endured for over a millennium. Butter tea is the universal gesture of Tibetan hospitality — a host will refill your cup the moment you set it down, and a guest's cup is never allowed to run dry. It is served in monasteries, nomad tents, and city homes alike.

How to Drink:

  1. Drink it hot, in small sips, savoring the warm, savory richness
  2. Accept refills freely — it is impolite for a host to leave your cup empty
  3. Pair it with tsampa, the classic Tibetan combination
  4. Take it easy at first if the salty, buttery flavor is unfamiliar — it grows on you

Price Range: ¥5–15 for a pot at a tea house; refills are often generous

Where to Try:

  • Traditional sweet tea houses (甜茶馆) throughout the Barkhor area
  • Tibetan family restaurants — order a pot alongside your meal
  • Monastery tea houses — a peaceful place to sample it amid pilgrims

Folk Culture

Tibetan Buddhism & the Pilgrimage (藏传佛教与朝圣文化)

Origin & History: Lhasa is the sacred capital of Tibetan Buddhism, and its very name — meaning "place of the gods" — reflects a faith that has shaped every stone of the city. Buddhism arrived on the plateau in the 7th century under King Songtsen Gampo, who built the Jokhang Temple and, according to tradition, the first Potala. Over the following centuries, the religion fused with indigenous Bon beliefs to form the distinctive Vajrayana Buddhism of Tibet, organized around great monastic universities like Sera, Ganden, and Drepung. At its heart is the practice of pilgrimage: devout Tibetans travel vast distances — some prostrating their bodies along the entire route — to circumambulate the Jokhang and the Potala, spin prayer wheels, and accumulate spiritual merit. The belief that a single journey to Lhasa can transform a life has made the city the axis of one of the world's great religious civilizations.

Performance/Experience Form:

  • The Kora (Circumambulation) — walk clockwise around the Jokhang and Potala, joining the river of pilgrims with their prayer wheels and beads
  • Full-Body Prostrations — pilgrims perform thousands of prostrations, a profound expression of devotion seen outside the Jokhang daily
  • Yak-Butter Lamps — worshippers pour melted yak butter into brass lamps as offerings, filling the temples with warm light
  • Prayer Flags & Mani Stones — colorful lungta flags and carved "Om Mani Padme Hum" stones mark every pass and temple

Where to Experience:

  • The Jokhang Temple and Barkhor Street — the living center of Tibetan devotion
  • The Potala Palace — join the pilgrims circling its base in the early morning
  • Sera Monastery — witness the afternoon debates and the monks' daily rituals

Best Time to Experience: Early morning (before 9:00 AM), when the Jokhang and Barkhor are filled with pilgrims and the air is thick with juniper incense; and during major festivals such as the Monlam Great Prayer Festival

Shoton Festival (雪顿节 / The Yogurt Festival)

Origin & History: The Shoton Festival — literally the "Yogurt Banquet Festival" — is Lhasa's grandest celebration and one of Tibet's most beloved holidays. Its roots lie in the monastic calendar: during the summer retreat, when monks stayed indoors to avoid harming insects, laypeople would bring them yogurt as an offering at its close. Over time, this simple act of generosity grew into a week-long festival, and by the 17th century it had become the occasion for grand public performances of Tibetan opera (Lhamo). Today Shoton falls in late summer (usually August, in the sixth month of the Tibetan calendar) and draws pilgrims and visitors from across the plateau, who come for the unveiling of a giant thangka, the opera performances, and the sharing of yogurt that gives the festival its name.

Performance/Experience Form:

  • The Giant Thangka Unveiling — at dawn at Drepung Monastery, a colossal embroidered Buddha image is unfurled down the hillside before a sea of worshippers
  • Tibetan Opera (Lhamo) — troupes in vivid costumes and masks perform the traditional epic dramas in Norbulingka's parks
  • Yogurt Feasting — families share bowls of fresh yak yogurt, the festival's namesake treat
  • Picnics in Norbulingka — the summer palace gardens fill with families, food, singing, and dance

Where to Experience:

  • Drepung Monastery — the pre-dawn thangka unveiling, the festival's spiritual climax
  • Norbulingka (the Summer Palace) — the main venue for Tibetan opera and picnics
  • Across Lhasa — the whole city joins in, from Barkhor to the riverside parks

Best Time to Experience: The festival spans about a week each August; the thangka unveiling at Drepung begins before dawn, so plan to arrive very early to secure a good vantage point

Travel Tips

Best Time to Visit

High Season (May–October): This is the best window for most travelers — clearer skies, milder temperatures (daytime highs of 15–23°C), and the great summer festivals, including the Shoton Festival in August. Days are warm and bright, though nights remain cool. This is also when permits and tickets are in highest demand, so book well ahead.

Shoulder & Off Season (November–April): Winter in Lhasa is cold but often brilliantly sunny — daytime temperatures can reach 10°C under intense sun, though nights drop below freezing. There are far fewer tourists, tickets are discounted, and the city is at its most serene, but some outlying routes and attractions have limited access. Avoid the deepest cold if you are sensitive to altitude.

Avoid:

  • Arriving and immediately attempting strenuous sightseeing — altitude sickness is real at 3,650 meters
  • Late-autumn snowstorms can disrupt flights and mountain roads; check conditions in advance

Recommended Duration

Minimum: 3 full days (Day 1: acclimatize gently + Barkhor & Jokhang; Day 2: Potala Palace + a tea house; Day 3: Sera Monastery debates)

Ideal: 4–5 days — add Norbulingka, a morning on the Potala kora, and a slower, deeper exploration of the old city's lanes and temples

Extended: 6–8 days — add day trips or overnight excursions to Namtso Lake, Yamdrok Lake, or the monasteries of Drepung and Ganden for the full plateau experience

Getting Around the City

Walking (Recommended): Lhasa's old city is compact and best explored on foot — the Jokhang, Barkhor, and many guesthouses are within easy walking distance. At altitude, simply walk slowly and rest often.

Taxi: Cheap and plentiful within the city — flag fall is ¥10, and most trips across the old city cost ¥10–15. Drivers generally use the meter for central journeys.

Bus: An extensive network (¥1–2 per ride) connects the Potala, Sera, Drepung, and Norbulingka; route numbers are clearly marked, but destinations are usually in Chinese only.

Rideshare: DiDi operates in Lhasa and is useful for reaching the monasteries on the city's edge.

Intercity Transport

Lhasa Gonggar Airport (LXA): About 60 km southwest of the city. Airport shuttle buses (¥30, roughly 60–70 minutes) run to the city center; taxi about ¥150–200. Direct flights connect Lhasa with Chengdu, Xi'an, Chongqing, Beijing, and other major cities — flying into Lhasa is the most common way in.

Qinghai–Tibet Railway:

  • Lhasa Railway Station is about 8 km southwest of the city center; taxi about ¥20–30
  • From Xining: the classic gateway route, about 20–22 hours, with spectacular plateau scenery; soft sleeper is recommended
  • Direct trains also run from Beijing, Chengdu, Chongqing, Shanghai, and Guangzhou, taking 30–50 hours

Practical Tips

Altitude & Health: Lhasa sits at 3,650 meters (12,000 feet), and mild altitude sickness (headache, breathlessness, poor sleep) is common for the first day or two. Acclimatize by resting on arrival, drinking plenty of water, avoiding alcohol and strenuous exercise for the first 24–48 hours, and climbing stairs slowly. If symptoms worsen, descend and seek medical help. Many travelers find acetazolamide useful — consult a doctor before traveling.

Permits (Foreign Visitors): Foreign travelers require a Tibet Travel Permit, which can only be obtained through a licensed tour operator as part of an organized tour. Apply well in advance, and carry your passport and permit at all times — they are checked at airport security, train stations, and major sites. Chinese citizens do not need this permit.

Money:

  • Alipay and WeChat Pay are widely accepted in shops and restaurants; link your card before arriving
  • Carry some cash (¥100–300) for tea houses, small stalls, and monastery donations

Sun & Weather: The sun at altitude is intense — bring high-SPF sunscreen, sunglasses, a hat, and lip balm even on cool days. Temperatures swing sharply between day and night, so dress in layers.

Language:

  • Tibetan and Mandarin are both spoken; Mandarin is widely understood in the city
  • English is limited outside major hotels — download an offline translation app
  • Learning "Tashi Delek" (the Tibetan greeting of good wishes) is warmly appreciated

Etiquette:

  • Always walk clockwise around temples, stupas, and the kora; never touch or climb on sacred objects
  • Ask before photographing people, especially pilgrims and monks; never photograph inside chapels
  • Remove your hat and speak quietly inside temples; do not point your feet at altars or religious images
  • Show respect when witnessing prostrations and prayer — this is a living faith, not a performance
