Xiamen

The Garden on the Sea — Where Colonial Charm Meets Coastal Soul

Core Scenic Spots

Gulangyu Island (Gulangyu)

Location: 500 meters off Xiamen's southwest coast, a 5-minute ferry ride from Lundu Ferry Terminal

Historical Background: Gulangyu Island is a UNESCO World Heritage site and Xiamen's most treasured destination — a car-free, 1.91-square-kilometer island where every street feels like a walk through a living museum of 19th-century colonial architecture, tropical gardens, and piano music. Following the Treaty of Nanjing in 1842, Xiamen (then Amoy) became one of China's first treaty ports, and Gulangyu was designated the foreign settlement. Over the next century, British, French, Dutch, Japanese, American, and Southeast Asian merchants built consulates, churches, hospitals, and lavish villas in a bewildering architectural mashup: Victorian Gothic next to Fujianese courtyard houses, Art Deco beside neoclassical colonnades. The island earned its nickname "Piano Island" because pianos became status symbols among returning overseas Chinese families — at its peak, Gulangyu had the highest per-capita piano ownership in China, and it remains home to China's only piano museum. Today, Gulangyu has no cars, no bicycles (except official electric carts), and over 1,000 preserved historic buildings connected by winding lanes overhung with bougainvillea and banyan trees.

Highlights:

  • Sunlight Rock (Riguangyan) — the island's highest point at 92 meters, with 360° panoramic views of the entire island, Xiamen skyline, and the Taiwan Strait beyond
  • Shuzhuang Garden — a classical Chinese garden built by a wealthy Taiwanese merchant in 1913, ingeniously designed to borrow ocean views and changing tides as part of its landscape composition; contains the Gulangyu Piano Museum
  • Organ Museum (Fengqin Bowuguan) — housed in the striking Octagon Building, displays over 100 reed and pipe organs from around the world, with daily live organ performances
  • Gulangyu International Settlement architecture walk — wander Longtou Road and the surrounding lanes to see the former British, French, and Japanese consulates, the Trinity Church, and countless art-deco villas with peeling plaster and bougainvillea-draped balconies
  • Longtou Road — the island's main commercial street, lined with seafood snack stalls, pearl shops, tea houses, and souvenir stores in restored colonial-era buildings

Opening Hours: Island accessible daily; Sunlight Rock 6:00 AM – 6:30 PM; Shuzhuang Garden 7:00 AM – 5:30 PM; Organ Museum 8:15 AM – 5:30 PM

Tickets: Ferry ¥35 round-trip (peak), ¥30 (off-peak); Sunlight Rock ¥60; Shuzhuang Garden ¥30; combo ticket (5 major sites) ¥100

Transportation: Ferry from Lundu Terminal (Lujiang Road, Xiamen Island) to Sanqiutian Wharf (Gulangyu). Metro Line 1 to Zhenhai Road Station, 10-minute walk to ferry terminal. Ferry tickets require advance booking during holidays via WeChat "厦门轮渡有限公司".

Best Duration: 1 full day for highlights; 2 days for a relaxed exploration including overnight stay at a boutique guesthouse

Best Season: Spring (March–May) when flowers bloom across the island; Autumn (October–November) for clear skies and comfortable ocean breezes. Avoid Chinese national holidays (May 1, Oct 1–7) when the island becomes extremely crowded and ferry tickets sell out weeks in advance.

Tips: Take the first ferry (7:10 AM) to experience the island before the crowds arrive. Wear comfortable walking shoes — the island is entirely walkable but has many steep lanes and steps. Bring a jacket — the sea breeze can be cool even in summer. Stay overnight at one of the colonial-era guesthouses for a magical evening when day-trippers have left and the island's piano-studded silence returns.

Nanputuo Temple (Nanputuo Si)

Location: No. 515 Siming South Road, at the foot of Wulao Mountain, adjacent to Xiamen University

Historical Background: Nanputuo Temple is one of the most important Buddhist monasteries in southern Fujian, tracing its origins to the Tang Dynasty (618–907 AD) when a simple shrine was established on this site. The temple reached its current scale during the Qing Dynasty under the Kangxi Emperor, and its name — "Southern Putuo" — reflects its dedication to Guanyin, the Bodhisattva of Compassion, and its geographical position as a southern counterpart to the famed Putuo Mountain in Zhejiang. The temple complex unfolds across multiple terraces climbing the slope of Wulao Mountain: the Hall of Heavenly Kings, the Mahavira Hall, the Hall of Great Compassion, and the Sutra Depository, all connected by stone stairways shaded by ancient banyan trees. The temple is still an active monastery — monks in gray robes chant sutras in the halls, incense smoke drifts through the courtyards, and the vegetarian restaurant serves meals prepared according to centuries-old Buddhist recipes. Behind the temple, a network of hiking trails ascends Wulao Mountain, where granite boulders are carved with centuries of calligraphy inscriptions by monks and visiting scholars.

Highlights:

  • Mahavira Hall — the temple's spiritual heart, housing three golden Buddha statues from the Ming Dynasty, surrounded by intricate wood carvings of Buddhist stories
  • Hall of Great Compassion — dedicated to the thousand-armed Guanyin, with magnificent painted ceilings and carved pillars
  • Nanputuo Vegetarian Restaurant — one of China's most famous temple vegetarian kitchens, serving elaborate multi-course feasts that recreate classic Fujian dishes using tofu, mushrooms, and wheat gluten (from ¥80/person)
  • Wulao Mountain hiking trail — climb 185 meters through massive granite boulders inscribed with ancient calligraphy, reaching a summit with views across Xiamen University, the South China Sea, and on clear days, the islands of Kinmen (Jinmen)
  • Sutra Repository — houses rare Buddhist texts and a collection of blood-written sutras by devout monks

Opening Hours: Outer courtyard 3:00 AM – 6:00 PM; inner halls 8:00 AM – 5:00 PM

Tickets: Free entry to temple grounds and main halls; Wulao Mountain trail free

Transportation: Bus routes 1, 21, 45, 751, 841 to Nanputuo Station. Adjacent to the main gate of Xiamen University (west gate) — easily combined with a campus walk.

Best Duration: 1.5–2 hours for temple; 3–4 hours including Wulao Mountain hike and vegetarian lunch

Best Season: Year-round; early morning (before 9 AM) for peaceful temple atmosphere before tour groups arrive; spring (March–April) when phoenix flowers bloom on the mountain trail

Tips: Dress modestly — cover shoulders and knees. Photography is allowed in courtyards but not inside the main halls during ceremonies. The vegetarian restaurant is genuinely excellent — try the "Buddha Jumps Over the Wall" vegetarian version (mock meats in a rich mushroom broth). Combine with Xiamen University (free entry with passport registration at the gate) for a full cultural half-day.

Huandao Road (Island Ring Road)

Location: A 31-kilometer coastal boulevard encircling the southeastern edge of Xiamen Island, from Xiamen University to the Wutong Ferry Terminal

Historical Background: Huandao Road was built in the late 1990s as part of Xiamen's transformation from a sleepy port city into a modern coastal metropolis. Unlike typical urban highways, Huandao Road was conceived as a scenic corridor — a palm-lined ribbon of asphalt hugging the coastline, with dedicated cycling paths, beach access points, sculpture parks, and observation platforms spaced along its length. The road passes through some of Xiamen's most dramatic coastal geography: golden sand beaches framed by granite outcrops, mangrove-fringed bays, and rocky headlands from which you can see the islands of Kinmen just 10 kilometers across the water. The road itself became an attraction — it has been repeatedly voted China's most beautiful urban road, hosts the annual Xiamen International Marathon (first weekend of January), and serves as Xiamen's communal backyard, where families picnic, couples take wedding photos at sunset, and cyclists from around the world tackle its oceanfront curves.

Highlights:

  • Zengcuo'an Village (served as a separate stop below) — the most vibrant section of the road, filled with guesthouses, cafes, and seafood BBQ stalls in a former fishing village
  • Huangcuo Beach — Xiamen's cleanest and least crowded beach, with soft golden sand and gentle waves suitable for swimming (May–October)
  • Music Square — an oceanfront plaza with musical-themed sculptures, live street performances on weekends, and views toward Kinmen Island
  • "One Country, Two Systems" viewing platform — a boardwalk plaza facing Kinmen, where large binoculars offer a close look at the Taiwan-administered islands with their distinctive signage visible on clear days
  • Yefengzhai (Barbarian Fort) — a restored Ming Dynasty coastal defense fort with a zip-line and summer water park, built to repel pirates

Opening Hours: Road accessible 24 hours; beach areas and parks generally 6:00 AM – 10:00 PM

Tickets: Road and beaches free; Yefengzhai ¥25

Transportation: Bus route 29 runs the full length of Huandao Road (¥1). Bike rental widely available — ¥20–40/day for a standard bike, ¥30–50/day for electric bike. DiDi from city center approximately ¥25.

Best Duration: 2–3 hours by electric bike for highlights; full day by bicycle for a complete loop with beach stops

Best Season: Spring and autumn for comfortable cycling weather; summer evenings after 5 PM when the sun softens over the ocean; January for the marathon atmosphere

Tips: Rent an electric bike — the full 31 km loop is beautiful but too long for most to cycle comfortably in humid Xiamen heat. Start from Zengcuo'an in the late afternoon and ride east toward Huangcuo Beach, timing your return to arrive at sunset. Bring swimwear in summer — Huangcuo Beach is genuinely swimmable. The section near Xiamen University is the most photogenic at golden hour.

Zhongshan Road Pedestrian Street (Zhongshan Lu)

Location: Zhongshan Road, Siming District — stretching 1.2 km from Lujiang Road in the west to Xinhua Road in the east

Historical Background: Zhongshan Road is Xiamen's oldest commercial artery — a continuous, covered arcade street (qilou) of the type built throughout southern Chinese treaty ports and Southeast Asian colonial cities. The arcade design, with its continuous ground-floor colonnade sheltering pedestrians from both tropical sun and sudden monsoon downpours, was brought to Xiamen by returning overseas Chinese merchants who had seen similar streets in Singapore, Penang, and Manila. Construction began in the 1920s, and by the 1930s Zhongshan Road had become the commercial heart of Amoy, lined with silk shops, tea merchants, banks, pharmacies, and the city's first department stores. Many of these original businesses are still operating in the same buildings — the Huang Zehe Peanut Soup Shop has been serving its famous sweet soup since 1945, and the 1980 Roast Meat Zongzi stall still draws queues. In 2006, the street was converted to a pedestrian mall, and today it is Xiamen's most popular evening destination — a 1.2-kilometer stretch of restored neoclassical facades glowing with neon signs, street food stalls, and the constant hum of a city out for the night.

Highlights:

  • Night market atmosphere — the street truly comes alive after 6 PM, when food stalls set up, shop lights illuminate the arcades, and the entire length fills with locals and visitors
  • Huang Zehe Peanut Soup Shop — a Xiamen institution since 1945, serving fragrant sweet peanut soup (¥5) and dozens of traditional Fujianese pastries and snacks (cash-only token system)
  • 1980 Roast Meat Zongzi — arguably Xiamen's best zongzi (glutinous rice dumplings), served with satay sauce and chili paste (¥8), from the same family recipe since 1980
  • Arcade architecture — look up to appreciate the detailed plaster facades, carved balustrades, and stained glass transom windows above the shopfronts
  • Side lanes — duck into the narrow alleys branching off Zhongshan Road (Jukou Street, Dazhong Road) to discover local seafood stalls, old-school tea houses, and family-run oyster omelette shops

Opening Hours: Shops generally 10:00 AM – 10:00 PM; food stalls active from late afternoon to midnight

Tickets: Free; pedestrian street

Transportation: Metro Line 1 to Zhenhai Road Station (Exit 1), 5-minute walk. Bus routes 1, 3, 8, 10, 11, 19, 21, 32 to Zhongshan Road Station. The street's west end connects to the Lundu Ferry Terminal (for Gulangyu).

Best Duration: 2–3 hours for food and shopping; evening visit (6 PM – 9 PM) for the best atmosphere

Best Season: Year-round; evenings in autumn and spring are most pleasant for walking; summer evenings are hot but lively

Tips: Go hungry — the street food is the attraction. Huang Zehe uses a token system: buy a prepaid card at the counter, pay with tokens at stalls, refund unused balance. Combine with dinner at a seafood stall in the side lanes. The street connects directly to the Gulangyu ferry terminal — convenient to visit both on the same day.

Featured Cuisine

Satay Noodles (沙茶面 / Sha Cha Mian)

Flavor Profile: A bowl of al dente wheat noodles suspended in a burnt-orange broth of extraordinary depth — simultaneously spicy, nutty, faintly sweet, and suffused with the savory essence of dried shrimp and the sea. The soul of the dish is sha cha sauce, a uniquely Fujianese condiment born from Xiamen's centuries-old spice trade with Southeast Asia. Made from yellow soybeans, dried shrimp, garlic, shallots, coconut powder, chili, and a trace of peanut — all pounded into a paste and slow-sautéed until its raw edges dissolve — sha cha sauce is then simmered into a pork bone broth until the liquid turns thick and rust-colored. Diners choose their toppings from a glass case: fresh shrimp, squid rings, pork liver, tofu puffs, blood curd, fish balls, sliced pork, leafy greens — each addition priced separately, creating infinite combinations. A scatter of cilantro, fried shallots, and a spoonful of garlic paste finish the bowl. Every neighborhood in Xiamen has its own sha cha mian shop, and each guards its sha cha recipe as a family secret.

Historical & Cultural Story: Sha cha sauce is Xiamen's most delicious souvenir of its treaty-port past. When Indonesian and Malay Chinese returned to Fujian in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, they brought back the concept of satay — skewered, grilled meat served with peanut sauce. Local cooks adapted the satay sauce to local ingredients (replacing peanuts partly with soybeans and adding dried shrimp for Fujianese umami) and paired it with noodles instead of skewers. The result was sha cha mian — a dish that tastes like the story of Xiamen itself: a Chinese foundation enriched by Southeast Asian influences, built by returning diaspora merchants, and perfected over generations of family-run shops. Wu Zaitian near Zhongshan Road has been serving the same sha cha recipe since 1945, and its morning queues stretch around the block.

How to Eat:

  1. Pick your toppings first — the noodles are the constant, the toppings are your self-portrait
  2. Stir thoroughly — the sha cha sediment settles, and every bite should be evenly coated
  3. Slurp loudly — this is a working-class dish; ceremony is for the soup spoons, not the noodles
  4. Finish the broth — it carries the accumulated flavor of all the toppings simmered within

Price Range: ¥10–15 for a basic bowl; ¥20–35 with premium toppings (shrimp, squid, extra meats)

Where to Try:

  • Wu Zaitian (乌糖沙茶面) on Minzu Road — the most famous, open since 1945, but sells out by 1 PM
  • Yuehua Satay Noodles (月华沙茶面) near Zhongshan Road — a local favorite since 1985, authentic and generous
  • Any neighborhood shop with a morning queue — the best endorsement in Xiamen is a line of locals holding steaming bowls

Oyster Omelette (海蛎煎 / Hai Li Jian)

Flavor Profile: A golden, crisped-edged pancake that sizzles onto the plate in a fragrant cloud of hot oil and brine. Tiny Xiamen oysters — smaller and sweeter than their northern cousins, harvested from the coastal mudflats just offshore — are folded into a batter of sweet potato starch that delivers the dish's trademark dual texture: papery-crisp edges that shatter between the teeth, and a soft, almost translucent chewy center where the oysters nestle like hidden jewels. Duck eggs, richer and more golden than chicken eggs, are beaten in for structure, while chopped scallions and garlic chives add a bright green sharpness. The finished omelette is served with a puddle of tangy-sweet chili sauce — a Fujianese condiment of red chilies, garlic, and rice vinegar — that cuts through the richness and amplifies the oceanic flavor of the oysters.

Historical & Cultural Story: Hai Li Jian was born of coastal poverty and resourcefulness. In the fishing villages dotting Fujian's jagged coastline, oysters were abundant and cheap, while rice was scarce and expensive. Sweet potato starch — readily available from the terraced fields inland — became the clever binder that could stretch a handful of oysters into a meal for a hungry family. The dish spread with Fujianese emigrants across the Taiwan Strait (where it became a night-market staple in Taipei and Tainan) and across the South China Sea to Singapore and Malaysia's hawker centers. But Xiamen remains its spiritual home — this is where the oysters are freshest, the griddles are seasoned by decades of use, and the recipe has been passed down through five generations of street vendors.

How to Eat:

  1. Eat immediately — the crisp edges soften within minutes
  2. Dip generously in the chili sauce — the sweet-tangy-spicy contrast is essential
  3. Alternate bites with pickled radish or cucumber on the side to cleanse the palate
  4. Order one per person — sharing an oyster omelette in Xiamen is considered poor form

Price Range: ¥12–20 per serving

Where to Try:

  • Lianhuan Oyster Omelette (莲欢海蛎煎) in Jukou Street — a tiny, family-run shop operating since the 1980s, widely considered the gold standard
  • Zhongshan Road night market — dozens of competing stalls, quality varies but the sizzling atmosphere is unbeatable
  • Zengcuo'an Village — touristy but scenic, stall-hopping along the seaside promenade

Ginger Duck (姜母鸭 / Jiang Mu Ya)

Flavor Profile: A clay pot of profound, warming comfort — fall-apart-tender duck braised in an avalanche of caramelized ginger and aged sesame oil until the meat achieves a texture somewhere between confit and stew, and the kitchen fills with the heady, almost medicinal fragrance of ginger and sesame. The dish uses an extraordinary quantity of ginger — often an entire hand's worth, sliced into thick coins — which is first fried in black sesame oil until golden and crisp-edged, releasing its volatile oils into the fat. Bone-in duck pieces are seared in the ginger-sesame oil, then braised with rice wine, soy sauce, rock sugar, and a sachet of warm spices (star anise, cinnamon, Sichuan pepper) until the meat is spoon-tender and the sauce reduces to a dark, glossy glaze. The ginger itself becomes an ingredient rather than aromatics — the fried coins are sweet, chewy, and intensely aromatic, and Xiamen locals argue that eating the ginger is as important as eating the duck.

Historical & Cultural Story: Ginger Duck is Fujian's winter tonic — a dish that sits at the intersection of food and traditional Chinese medicine. In Fujianese wellness philosophy, duck is a "cooling" (yin) food, while ginger and sesame oil are intensely "warming" (yang). The combination creates a balanced dish that is believed to dispel dampness, strengthen the body, and warm the blood during the humid, chilly winters of the southern coast. The dish likely originated in Quanzhou, just north of Xiamen, during the Song Dynasty, when Fujianese merchants returning from Southeast Asia brought back both the technique of clay-pot cooking and a taste for richly spiced food. Today, Ginger Duck shops are a fixture of Xiamen's winter streets — recognizable from blocks away by the scent of frying ginger that pours from their doors.

How to Eat:

  1. Start with the ginger — the fried slices are a delicacy, eaten alongside the duck
  2. Use the sauce to dress steamed rice — the sesame-ginger-soy reduction is the true prize
  3. Best eaten communally from a large clay pot shared among 3–4 people with several side dishes
  4. Pair with a cold Tsingtao beer or warm Shaoxing wine — both cut through the richness

Price Range: ¥48–88 for a half duck (serves 2); ¥88–158 for a whole duck (serves 3–4)

Where to Try:

  • A Qiang Ginger Duck (阿强姜母鸭) on Hubin South Road — the most famous, with multiple branches
  • Tong'an Fengrou (同安封肉) shops — many traditional Fujianese restaurants serve excellent ginger duck alongside other regional specialties
  • Neighborhood clay-pot restaurants in Siming District — any shop with "姜母鸭" in its name and a line at lunchtime

Folk Culture

Minnan Culture & Overseas Chinese Heritage (闽南文化与侨乡)

Origin & History: Xiamen is the cultural capital of Minnan — the "southern Min" region encompassing southern Fujian, Taiwan, and the global diaspora of Hokkien-speaking communities across Southeast Asia. Minnan culture is distinct from the mainstream Han cultures of northern and central China: it speaks a language (Hokkien) that preserves features of Middle Chinese from a thousand years ago; it practices a form of folk religion blending Buddhism, Taoism, and ancestor worship centered on elaborate family shrines; its architecture favors red brick, swallowtail roof ridges, and intricate stone carvings; and its traditional arts — particularly the operatic form Nanyin and the hand-puppet theater Budaixi — are UNESCO-recognized intangible cultural heritage. Xiamen's position as a historic "qiaoxiang" (hometown of overseas Chinese) means that for over 400 years, Minnan culture has flowed outward with emigrants and returned enriched by their experiences in Taiwan, Singapore, the Philippines, and Indonesia.

Performance/Experience Form:

  • Nanyin (南音) — "southern music," an ancient form of chamber music performed with pipa, dongxiao flute, and a singer narrating classical poetry in Minnan dialect; designated a UNESCO Masterpiece of Oral and Intangible Heritage
  • Budaixi (布袋戏) — glove puppet theater, where elaborately costumed hand puppets act out historical epics and folk tales in Minnan dialect, accompanied by live percussion
  • Temple festivals — throughout the year, neighborhood temples celebrate the birthdays of patron deities with processions, firecrackers, lion dances, and street banquets where the entire community gathers at red-draped tables
  • Qilou (arcade) architecture — the distinctive covered walkways of Zhongshan Road and the old town, a physical expression of Minnan's hybrid Chinese-Southeast Asian urban culture

Where to Experience:

  • Nanputuo Temple during the Lantern Festival (15th day of the first lunar month) — one of Xiamen's grandest temple celebrations
  • Xiamen Nanyin Troupe at the Gulangyu Concert Hall — regular performances of this ancient musical form
  • Old town lanes around Zhongshan Road — peek into open doorways to see family ancestral shrines, the heart of Minnan domestic culture

Best Time to Experience: Lunar New Year (especially the 15th day, the Lantern Festival) for temple festivals; Mid-Autumn Festival for mooncake gambling (博饼), a uniquely Xiamen tradition where families and offices gamble for mooncakes by rolling dice

Bo Bing — The Mooncake Gambling Festival (中秋博饼)

Origin & History: Bo Bing (博饼, literally "gambling for cakes") is unique to Xiamen and the surrounding Minnan region — a 300-year-old Mid-Autumn tradition invented by Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga), the Ming Dynasty loyalist who used Xiamen as his base to resist the Qing and briefly ruled Taiwan. According to legend, Zheng's soldiers were homesick during the Mid-Autumn Festival, so he devised a dice game using mooncakes as prizes to lift morale. The game uses six dice rolled into a large porcelain bowl; combinations of specific numbers correspond to ranks in the imperial examination system, each awarding a different grade of mooncake. A single "four" on one die = Xiucai (entry-level scholar), four "fours" = Jinshi (the highest degree), and rolling four red "fours" plus either two "ones" — the ultimate combination — makes you the Zhuangyuan (top scholar) and winner of the grand prize, the largest mooncake.

Performance/Experience Form:

  • Every workplace, school, neighborhood, and family in Xiamen organizes its own Bo Bing gathering during the Mid-Autumn period (which can stretch for weeks before and after the actual lunar date)
  • The dice-bowl clatter is Xiamen's soundtrack during September and October — hotel lobbies, restaurant private rooms, and office conference rooms all echo with rolls and cheers
  • Modern prizes range from mooncakes to electric appliances, cash, and even cars at corporate events
  • The Zhuangyuan traditionally wears a red ribbon and is paraded around with drums and gongs at community events

Where to Experience:

  • Any large restaurant or hotel during September–October — many offer Bo Bing packages with dinner, mooncakes, and designated dice-rolling areas
  • Gulangyu Island — Zheng Chenggong's statue on Sunlight Rock is the spiritual home of the tradition
  • Zhongshan Road — stores and arcades host public Bo Bing events during the festival season

Best Time to Experience: Mid-Autumn Festival (15th day of the 8th lunar month, usually mid-September to early October); the festive atmosphere extends for 2–3 weeks around the actual date

Travel Tips

Best Time to Visit

Peak Season (Ideal): Spring (March–May) and Autumn (October–November). Spring brings mild temperatures (18–25°C), blooming bougainvillea across the city, and the famous Xiamen rain — brief, warm showers that clear to brilliant sunshine. Autumn is Xiamen's most glorious season: crystal-clear skies, comfortable humidity, and the Mid-Autumn Festival with its unique Bo Bing mooncake gambling tradition. Ocean temperatures remain swimmable through October.

Shoulder Season: December–February. Winters are mild by Chinese standards (10–18°C) but can be overcast and occasionally chilly with sea winds. Hotel prices drop and tourist sites are uncrowded — an excellent time for exploring the city without queues. The Chinese New Year period (late January/February) brings festive decorations, temple activities, and a surge of overseas Chinese returning home, but many small restaurants close for the holiday.

Avoid:

  • July–August — hot (30–35°C), humid, and prone to typhoons that can disrupt ferry service to Gulangyu; summer is also peak domestic tourism with crowded attractions and higher prices
  • Chinese National Day (Oct 1–7) — Gulangyu ferry tickets sell out weeks in advance, hotel prices triple, and popular spots are packed shoulder-to-shoulder
  • Typhoon season (July–September) — while Xiamen's infrastructure handles storms well, ferry service to Gulangyu and Kinmen may be suspended for 1–3 days, and outdoor attractions close during typhoon warnings

Recommended Duration

Minimum: 2 full days (Day 1: Gulangyu Island + Zhongshan Road evening; Day 2: Nanputuo Temple + Xiamen University + Huandao Road cycling afternoon)

Ideal: 3–4 days — add Zengcuo'an Village, a proper Sha Cha Mian breakfast crawl, the Overseas Chinese Museum, and a sunset seafood dinner on Huandao Road

Extended: 5–7 days — add day trips to the Tulou (Hakka earthen fortresses, UNESCO World Heritage, 2.5h by bus), Quanzhou (ancient maritime Silk Road port, 30min by high-speed rail), or the volcanic island of Zhangzhou

Getting Around the City

Metro (Recommended):

  • 3 lines covering Xiamen Island and connecting to Xiamen North Railway Station on the mainland; Line 1 serves the main tourist corridor (Zhongshan Road, Nanputuo, Xiamen University)
  • Cost: ¥2–7 per ride; pay via Alipay transport QR or WeChat mini-program "厦门地铁"; note that Line 1 has a brief above-ground segment crossing the sea with ocean views
  • Operating hours: 6:00 AM – 11:00 PM

BRT (Bus Rapid Transit): Elevated dedicated bus lanes crisscross the island — faster than regular buses, unaffected by traffic. ¥0.5–3 per ride. Useful for reaching Xiamen North Station and the airport area.

Bus: Extensive network, ¥1 per ride. Route 29 is the tourist lifeline — it runs the full length of Huandao Road and stops at Xiamen University, Nanputuo Temple, Zengcuo'an, and various beaches. Air-conditioned, accept Alipay/WeChat QR.

Ferry: The Gulangyu ferry is a separate system — advance booking essential during holidays. The public ferry between Xiamen Island's Pier 1 and Haicang (mainland district) offers a cheap scenic cruise (¥2, every 30 minutes).

Taxi/DiDi: Starting fare ¥10 (first 3 km), ¥2/km after. Most trips within the island cost ¥15–35. Xiamen taxis are abundant and drivers are generally honest. DiDi app works in English.

Shared Bikes: Hellobike and Meituan bikes everywhere — ¥1.5/30 min. Huandao Road has dedicated bike lanes and is Xiamen's premier cycling route.

Intercity Transport

Xiamen Gaoqi International Airport (XMN): On Xiamen Island, 12 km from the city center. Metro Line 1 does not currently reach the airport; BRT Line 1 connects the airport to the city center (¥1–3, 30 min). Taxi to Zhongshan Road area approximately ¥40–50 (25–35 min). Direct flights to major Asian cities (Singapore, Bangkok, Kuala Lumpur, Manila, Tokyo, Seoul) and all major Chinese cities. A new airport (Xiang'an International) is under construction on the mainland, expected to open by 2026.

High-Speed Rail:

  • Xiamen Railway Station (厦门站): On Xiamen Island, connected to Metro Line 3; serves most high-speed and conventional trains. Closest to city center (10 min taxi to Zhongshan Road)
  • Xiamen North Railway Station (厦门北站): On the mainland in Jimei District, connected to Metro Line 1; serves additional high-speed routes, 45–60 min from city center
  • To Fuzhou: 1.5 hours (¥93)
  • To Quanzhou: 30 minutes (¥30)
  • To Shenzhen: 3–3.5 hours (¥180)
  • To Shanghai: 6–7 hours (¥390)
  • To Hangzhou: 5 hours (¥330)
  • To Nanchang: 4 hours (¥210)

Practical Tips

Weather: Xiamen has a humid subtropical climate moderated by the ocean. The city is warm year-round with no true winter — January lows rarely dip below 10°C. Summer (June–September) is hot and humid with afternoon thunderstorms and typhoon risk. Spring (March–May) sees frequent drizzle. Autumn (October–November) is near-perfect. Always carry an umbrella in spring and summer.

Money:

  • Alipay/WeChat Pay accepted everywhere — link your international card before arriving
  • A few traditional food stalls on Gulangyu and in old neighborhoods are cash-only; carry ¥100–200 in small bills

Language:

  • Mandarin spoken everywhere; the local language is Minnan (Hokkien), still widely spoken among older residents and in traditional neighborhoods
  • English is more common in Xiamen than in most Chinese cities due to the tourism industry and overseas Chinese connections — hotel staff, museum signage, and many restaurant menus have English
  • Learn "沙茶面" (shā chá miàn) and "海蛎煎" (hǎi lì jiān) — these two dishes will define your trip

Safety:

  • Xiamen is one of China's safest cities — consistently ranked among the country's top cities for public safety
  • Beware of rip currents at Huangcuo Beach — swim in designated areas and obey lifeguard flags
  • Watch footing on Gulangyu — cobblestone lanes can be slippery after rain
  • Gulangyu's beaches look inviting but currents can be dangerous — stick to Xiamen Island beaches (Huangcuo, Baicheng) for swimming

Etiquette:

  • Xiamen people are famously warm and hospitable — the city's nickname "Garden on the Sea" reflects a gentler, slower pace than mainland Chinese megacities
  • Respect temple etiquette — dress modestly, speak quietly, and do not photograph monks without permission
  • No tipping culture; at street food stalls, exact change in cash is appreciated
  • Tea culture is central — if you visit a local shop or home, you will almost certainly be offered a cup of Tieguanyin (Iron Goddess of Mercy) oolong tea; accept it as a gesture of welcome
  • Many Gulangyu private villas are still residences — be respectful when photographing, and do not enter gated properties without invitation
